Olist Delivery Risk Analysis

Delivery risk lives in the tail, not the average. The data points to reliability — keeping the promise date — rather than raw speed as the lever for customer satisfaction.

BigQuery SQL · Power BI · Git/GitHub

An end-to-end analysis of ~100K Brazilian e-commerce orders: a BigQuery SQL pipeline, 8-step data profiling and a Power BI dashboard.

  • 6.77% of 96,476 delivered orders arrive late
  • 4.1 to 2.99 average review score when the promise is missed by 1 to 5 days
  • 2.8% to 21.4% late rate across states, from AM to AL

Q1. The average hides the tail

The median order arrives in 10 days. But the slowest 1% (~880 orders) took more than 46 days, and the worst took 209.

Key findings

Q3. Satisfaction drops off a cliff, not a slope

Arriving early barely beats arriving on time (4.3 vs 4.1). Crossing the promise date costs about 1.1 points.

96,476 delivered orders in total. The three late groups add up to the 6,535 late orders (6.77%).

Q2 and Q4. Lateness is regional, not product-driven

Q2, where lateness concentrates. The late rate ranges from 2.8% (AM) to 21.4% (AL). Among states with at least 500 delivered orders, the worst are MA (17.4%), CE (13.8%) and BA (12.2%). Volume misleads: SP has the most late orders (1,820) but only a 4.5% late rate, while RJ is at 12.1%.

Q4, category and seller risk. Product type matters far less. Most categories sit at 6–8% late, with audio the outlier at 11.6%. Seller state matters more: MA sellers are late on 19.4% of order-lines while every other state is at 7.1% or below. Geography drives delivery risk more than product type.

Q5. Stalling appears structural

2,965 orders never arrive: 619 canceled and 2,346 stuck. The stuck ones cluster at two stages, after approval and at the carrier. The stuck rate is flat by state (about 1.4–4%), so this is a process problem, not a regional one.

What this means

Lateness is regional, stalling is structural, and MA is the weak node on both the customer and seller side.

The operational target is to keep the promised date, not to be faster: beating the promise barely moves the review score (4.3 vs 4.1), while missing it by 1 to 5 days costs about 1.1 points.

Dataset and method

The Olist Brazilian E-Commerce dataset from Kaggle: 9 tables, ~100K orders, purchases from September 2016 to October 2018.

Pipeline

  1. olist_raw: the 9 Kaggle tables as loaded.
  2. olist_stg: cleaned, typed and standardized.
  3. olist_mart: 2 fact tables, fct_order_delivery (one row per order, 99,441) and fct_order_items (one row per order-line, 112,650).
  4. Power BI dashboard on top of the mart.

Every table was profiled on 8 checks: volume, primary key, nulls, cardinality, range, domain, referential integrity, business rules. 7 anomalies are logged (DQ-001 to DQ-007), each with a decision, none silently dropped.

SQL highlights

  • "Delivered" is defined by timestamp, not status. 8 orders are marked delivered with no delivery date. They stay in the table but drop out of the time-based questions.
  • Reviews are aggregated per order before joining. 547 orders carry multiple reviews, so a direct join would fan out. This was the one data issue that changed the mart design.
  • Orders are the spine, with left joins. 775 orders have no items. An inner join would drop them and bring back survivor bias.
  • Minimum-volume thresholds on category risk. Rates on tiny samples are noise, not risk, so categories need at least 250 order-lines.

Limitations

  • Correlation, not causation: the link between lateness and review scores is observed, not proven.
  • The category and seller analysis is measured at order-line level, not order level.
  • The stuck rate has no order-age cutoff, so recent orders still in transit may be counted as stuck.
  • Day counts are truncated to full 24-hour units, so "on-time" can include orders that arrived less than a day early.
